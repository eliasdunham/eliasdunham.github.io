MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Mar 2026 – Present

High-pressure thermal characterization apparatus

No apparatus existed to measure how candidate insulation materials behave at depth, so I designed and built one: a pressure vessel with an internal heating element and thermocouples, holding a sample at temperature under pressure while logging its heat loss.

Routing heater power and thermocouple leads through the pressure boundary was the hard part. Both had to survive submersion and pressurization, which impose different requirements, and waterproofing that holds at atmospheric pressure fails once water is forced into every seam. I worked through several sealing approaches before epoxy-potting the feedthroughs, which held at pressures equivalent to 104 fsw.

I built the test stack around the interface a wetsuit actually sees rather than bulk conductivity: a heated surface standing in for skin, the material under test, and water on the far side. It runs submerged and dry, which separates insulating performance from degradation by water intrusion. I wrote the pressurization, conditioning, and cool-down procedure and trained other lab members to run it.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb 2026 – Present

In-situ mixing syringe

I modeled a dual-barrel syringe in Fusion 360, including the barrel, plunger, and gaskets, with outlet geometry that confines mixing to the tip. Validated for delivery through a 23G needle.

The prototypes had recurring leaks in the same locations rather than randomly, which pointed at geometry rather than tolerance. I ran a stress concentration analysis on the sealing interface and found load concentrating at discrete points, leaving most of the gasket unloaded. Reshaping the interface to distribute sealing load eliminated the leak across eleven iterations.

Northeastern University · Nov–Dec 2024

Horse racing motion tracker

I drew the enclosure geometry in AutoCAD, cut the panels on a laser cutter, and integrated an accelerometer and LCD with an Arduino to read live acceleration and velocity for horse racing.

Getting numbers I could trust took longer than building the hardware. Raw acceleration is noisy and velocity has to be integrated from it, so uncorrected bias accumulates and the velocity estimate drifts. I tuned the filtering and integration parameters against a commercial reference sensor until the device tracked within 5% of it.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb–Apr 2026

Wearable stimulator housing

I designed and fabricated a clear SLA enclosure for an active stimulator carried on a live animal through in-vivo trials. It had to be wearable, sealed against fluid, and transparent enough to inspect the board without disassembly.

Fluid ingress was the recurring failure, entering at the wire ports and the lid seam. Both were tolerance problems: too loose admits fluid, too tight prevents the lid seating or cracks the resin during assembly. I converged on dimensions that sealed and still assembled by hand across roughly fifteen iterations, and contoured the base to the animal's back so the housing sat flat and loaded the seam evenly. It stayed sealed throughout the trials.

Northeastern University · Jan–Mar 2025

Biomimetic fan blade

I derived the blade geometry from owl wing structure for drone and HVAC use, adding ridges across the blade surface that imitate the serrations on owl feathers.

My first prototypes were not quiet enough. I had sized the ridges by eye, and serration geometry governs the effect far more than the presence of the feature does, so I selected amplitude and wavelength from published parametric aeroacoustic data instead.

That data also corrected the mechanism I had assumed. The serrated surface does not break turbulence up. It establishes a localized pressure pattern that generates coherent vortex structures persisting longer than ordinary turbulent eddies. At low speed these vortices periodically lift the airflow away from the blade surface, reducing the harmonic loading that produces tonal noise. At high speed they resist collapsing into the small dissipative eddies that produce broadband noise. Sizing the serrations accordingly reached a 30% noise reduction against a baseline blade in flow simulation.

Serration geometry informed by Wei et al., “Towards silent and efficient flight by combining bioinspired owl feather serrations with cicada wing geometry,” Nature Communications 15, 4337 (2024).

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Mar-Jun 2026

Electro-thermal actuation study

I characterized heater materials, cell chemistries, and shape-memory alloy springs to establish whether a millimeter-scale actuator could be driven by a power source small enough to sit alongside it.

My alloy activation temperatures came out inconsistent, but the inconsistency was patterned rather than random, which pointed at the fixture rather than the material. The fixture was adding friction that opposed the transition, so the alloy read as actuating later and more weakly than it does. I rebuilt it and recovered valid data. I also mapped nichrome resistance against power across several lengths and voltages and verified the results against Joule-heating theory.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb–Jun 2026

Cell delivery catheter

A catheter for intravenous cell delivery was not achieving the expected flow. Working along the central channel, I found a taper partway down its length acting as a bottleneck, and holding the channel at a constant diameter restored flow in one redesign.

I then manufactured the complete device sets the team ran experiments on: 40 assembled devices and roughly 320 individual parts, produced on an SLA printer and a laser cutter with a rotary axis to hold tolerance on the round features.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Mar 2026 – Present

High-pressure thermal characterization apparatus

The lab needed to know how candidate insulation materials behave at depth, and there was no apparatus that could tell us. So I built one: a pressure vessel with a heating element and thermocouples inside it, which lets me hold a sample at temperature under pressure and log how it loses heat.

Almost every hard problem came down to making electricity and water coexist. The heater and thermocouples had to survive submersion and pressurization, which are not the same requirement. Waterproofing that holds at atmospheric pressure fails once water is being actively forced into every seam. Sealing the feedthroughs through the tank wall was the worst of it, and after several approaches leaked I ended up potting the penetrations in epoxy, which finally held.

The other question was what we were actually simulating. A wetsuit's job is to sit between skin and cold water, so measuring bulk conductivity would have missed the point. The test stack had to reproduce that interface: a heated surface standing in for skin, the material under test, and water on the other side. The apparatus runs both submerged and dry, which lets us separate how well a material insulates from how much it is compromised by water intrusion. Once it held pressure reliably I wrote the standardized procedure for pressurization, conditioning, and cool-down, and trained other people in the lab to run it.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb 2026 – Present

In-situ mixing syringe

This started as a modeling problem and turned into a sealing problem. The device is a dual-barrel syringe that keeps two components isolated until they meet at the tip, so I modeled the barrel, plunger, and gaskets in Fusion 360 and shaped the outlet so mixing happens only where it should.

It leaked. The obvious explanation was that the gaskets were undersized, but the leak kept reappearing in the same places rather than randomly, which pointed at geometry instead of tolerance. I ran a stress concentration analysis across the sealing interface and found the load was piling up at specific points instead of distributing around the seal, so those points were the only ones actually doing any sealing. Reshaping the interface to spread the load across the whole gasket fixed it. Eleven iterations from the first model to a version that held.

Northeastern University · Nov–Dec 2024

Horse racing motion tracker

A wearable that reads out live acceleration and velocity for horse racing. I laid the enclosure out in AutoCAD, cut the panels on a laser cutter, and wired the accelerometer and LCD to an Arduino.

The hardware was the easy half. Getting numbers out of a raw accelerometer that I would actually trust took most of the project. Acceleration comes in noisy, and velocity has to be integrated from it, so any bias or noise you fail to remove accumulates and the velocity reading gradually walks away from reality. I spent the bulk of my time tuning the filtering and integration parameters in the code, checking each change against a commercial third-party sensor, until we tracked within 5% of it.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb–Apr 2026

Wearable stimulator housing

An active stimulator had to ride on a live animal through in-vivo trials, which meant the enclosure needed to be comfortable enough to wear, sealed against fluid, and clear enough to inspect the board without opening it up.

Fluid ingress was the failure I kept running into. There were two paths in, the wire ports and the lid seam, and both came down to tolerance: too loose and fluid gets through, too tight and the lid won't seat or the resin cracks on assembly. Finding dimensions that sealed and still went together by hand took roughly fifteen iterations. I also contoured the base to the animal's back so the housing sat flat instead of rocking, which matters both for the animal and for keeping the seam loaded evenly. It stayed sealed and working through the trials.

Northeastern University · Jan–Mar 2025

Biomimetic fan blade

Owls are the reference case for quiet flight, so I used their wing structure as the starting point for a fan blade aimed at drone and HVAC use, adding ridges across the blade surface in imitation of the serrations on owl feathers.

The first prototypes were not quiet enough. The ridges were there, but I had picked their dimensions by eye, and it turns out the geometry matters far more than the presence of the feature. So I went to the literature and found a study that had run a parametric sweep of serration amplitude and wavelength on bioinspired propellers, and used it to choose values instead of guessing.

It also corrected how I understood the mechanism. I had assumed the ridges worked by breaking turbulence up. In fact the serrated surface sets up a localized pressure pattern that generates coherent vortex structures which persist longer than ordinary turbulent eddies. At low speeds those structures lift flow away from the blade surface and reduce the harmonic loading that produces tonal noise. At high speeds they resist collapsing into the small dissipative eddies that produce broadband noise. Serration density cuts both ways, too: dense patterns help at low Reynolds number and hurt at high. Choosing amplitude and wavelength with that in mind got me to a 30% reduction against a baseline blade in flow simulation.

Serration geometry informed by Wei et al., “Towards silent and efficient flight by combining bioinspired owl feather serrations with cicada wing geometry,” Nature Communications 15, 4337 (2024).

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · 2026

Electro-thermal actuation study

The question was whether a millimeter-scale actuator could be driven by a power source small enough to sit beside it. I worked through heater materials, cell chemistries, and shape-memory alloy springs to find where the feasible envelope actually was.

The measurement turned out to be the problem. My alloy activation temperatures kept coming out inconsistent, and the inconsistency had a pattern to it, which usually means the setup rather than the material. The fixture holding the spring was adding friction that opposed the transition, so the alloy looked like it actuated later and more weakly than it really does. Rebuilding the fixture recovered clean data. That one taught me to suspect my own apparatus before I suspect the sample. I also mapped nichrome resistance against power across several lengths and voltages and checked it against Joule-heating theory to confirm the heater side was behaving as expected.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb–Jun 2026

Cell delivery catheter

I picked up a catheter design for intravenous cell delivery that was not flowing the way it should. Working along the central channel, I found a taper partway down its length, a gradual narrowing nobody had flagged, acting as a bottleneck. Holding the channel at a constant diameter restored flow in a single redesign.

The rest of the project was volume. The team needed complete device sets to run experiments on, which came out to 40 assembled devices and roughly 320 individual parts, produced on an SLA printer and a laser cutter with a rotary axis to hold tolerance on the round features. Making the same part 40 times is its own discipline: a fixture that works once is not the same as a process that works forty times in a row.

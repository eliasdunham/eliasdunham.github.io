MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Mar 2026 – Present

High-pressure thermal characterization apparatus

No apparatus existed to measure how candidate insulation materials behave at depth. Bulk conductivity data existed at atmospheric pressure and nowhere else, and it would not have captured the interface a wetsuit actually sees between skin and cold water.

I designed and built a pressure vessel around that interface: a PID-controlled surface held at 37.2°C standing in for skin, the material under test, and water on the far side. Routing heater power and thermocouple leads through the pressure boundary was the hard part, since waterproofing that holds at atmospheric pressure fails once water is forced into every seam. I worked through several sealing approaches before epoxy-potting the feedthroughs.

The apparatus holds pressures equivalent to 104 fsw and runs submerged or dry, which separates insulating performance from degradation by water intrusion. I have used it to evaluate 24 candidate material formulations, and I wrote the pressurization, conditioning, and cool-down procedure that four other lab members now run independently.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb 2026 – Present

In-situ mixing syringe

A two-component formulation had to stay isolated until the moment of injection, which no single-barrel syringe can do.

I modeled a dual-barrel syringe in Fusion 360, including the barrel, plunger, and gaskets, with outlet geometry confining mixing to the tip. The prototypes leaked, and the leak recurred in the same locations rather than randomly, which pointed at geometry rather than tolerance. A stress concentration analysis of the sealing interface showed load concentrating at discrete points and leaving most of the gasket unloaded, so I reshaped the interface to distribute it.

Eleven SLA-printed iterations eliminated the leak. The device delivered through a 23G needle across eight consecutive injection trials without failure.

Northeastern University · Nov–Dec 2024

Horse racing motion tracker

The task was to measure acceleration and velocity on a moving horse and display both live, using a sensor whose raw output drifts.

I drew the enclosure geometry in AutoCAD, cut the panels on a laser cutter, and integrated an accelerometer and LCD with an Arduino. Getting numbers I could trust took longer than building the hardware. Raw acceleration is noisy and velocity has to be integrated from it, so uncorrected bias accumulates and the velocity estimate drifts. I tuned the filtering and integration parameters against a commercial reference sensor.

The finished device tracked within 5% of the reference across the range we tested.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb–Apr 2026

Wearable stimulator housing

An active stimulator carried on a live animal through in-vivo trials needed an enclosure that was wearable, sealed against fluid, and transparent enough to inspect the board without disassembly.

I designed and fabricated it in clear SLA resin, contouring the base to the animal's back so the housing sat flat and loaded the lid seam evenly. Fluid entered at the wire ports and the seam, and both were tolerance problems: too loose admits fluid, too tight prevents the lid seating or cracks the resin during assembly. I converged on dimensions that sealed and still assembled by hand.

Fifteen iterations produced a housing that stayed sealed and functional through four in-vivo trials.

Northeastern University · Jan–Mar 2025

Biomimetic fan blade

The goal was to reduce the noise a fan generates for drone and HVAC applications without sacrificing airflow.

I derived the blade geometry from owl wing structure and added ridges across the surface that imitate the serrations on owl feathers. My first prototypes were not quiet enough. I had sized the ridges by eye, and serration geometry governs the effect far more than the presence of the feature does, so I selected amplitude and wavelength from published parametric aeroacoustic data instead.

That data also corrected the mechanism I had assumed. The serrated surface does not break turbulence up. It establishes a localized pressure pattern that generates coherent vortex structures persisting longer than ordinary turbulent eddies. At low speed these vortices periodically lift the airflow away from the blade surface, reducing the harmonic loading that produces tonal noise. At high speed they resist collapsing into the small dissipative eddies that produce broadband noise. Serration density trades off between the two regimes, helping at low Reynolds number and hurting at high.

Sizing the serrations accordingly, I reached a 30% noise reduction against a baseline blade in flow simulation, a 40% improvement over my own first prototypes.

Serration geometry informed by Wei et al., “Towards silent and efficient flight by combining bioinspired owl feather serrations with cicada wing geometry,” Nature Communications 15, 4337 (2024).

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · 2026

Electro-thermal actuation study

The open question was whether a millimeter-scale actuator could be driven by a power source small enough to sit alongside it.

I characterized heater materials, cell chemistries, and shape-memory alloy springs to establish the feasible envelope. My alloy activation temperatures came out inconsistent, but the inconsistency was patterned rather than random, which pointed at the fixture rather than the material. The fixture was adding friction that opposed the transition, so the alloy read as actuating later and more weakly than it does. I rebuilt it.

The rebuilt fixture recovered valid activation data. I also mapped nichrome resistance against power across several lengths and voltages and verified the results against Joule-heating theory, which bounded the power envelope the actuator had to work within.

MIT Lab for Translational Engineering · Feb–Jun 2026

Cell delivery catheter

A catheter for intravenous cell delivery was not achieving the expected flow rate, which risks shearing the cells it is meant to deliver intact.

Working along the central channel, I found a taper partway down its length acting as a bottleneck. I redesigned the channel to reduce the taper and the flow impedance it created, holding a constant diameter through the length of the passage.

The redesign restored flow in a single revision. I then manufactured the complete device sets the team ran experiments on: 42 assembled devices and 336 printed and machined parts, produced on an SLA printer and a laser cutter with a rotary axis to hold tolerance on the round features.
